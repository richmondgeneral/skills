# Richmond General brand voice

## When to use

Anytime a written deliverable needs to sound like Richmond General. Examples:

- "Write a description for this item"
- "Draft a category intro for The Apothecary Cabinet"
- "Make this email on-brand"
- "What should the tagline be for the New Arrivals section?"
- "Rewrite this Etsy listing in our voice"
- "Caption these social photos"

## Voice descriptor

**Curated mercantile.**

Slightly archaic and intentional. Heritage- and craft-forward. Each item gets a small story; nothing is sold without provenance or attention to condition. Think small-town general store circa 1920, run by someone who knows their inventory and respects the buyer.

## Tonal rules

### Do

- **Specific over generic.** Name the era, the maker, the material. "1930s pressed-back oak" not "vintage chair."
- **Acknowledge condition honestly.** "Light edge wear consistent with age" beats "good condition." If something has a chip, say where.
- **Use mercantile language.** "Carefully selected," "newly arrived," "set aside for the next collector," "still bears its original label," "as found in an estate sale in Iowa."
- **Reference the era or cultural context.** "Like the love potions sold in Victorian general stores..." or "Hand-tied in the tradition of frontier healers." Anchor the item to a time and place.
- **Treat the buyer like a peer who knows their stuff.** Don't over-explain "Bakelite"  -  name it and trust them.
- **End with a practical note.** Use, care, gift potential, pairing suggestions. "Ideal for slow afternoons and group rituals"  -  gives the buyer permission to imagine using it.

### Don't

- **No hype words.** Avoid "amazing," "incredible," "must-have," "perfect," "stunning."
- **No corporate stock phrasing.** Avoid "buy now while supplies last," "limited time offer," "elevate your space."
- **No empty adjectives.** "Beautiful," "unique," "one-of-a-kind"  -  replace with specifics.
- **No call-to-action language ending lines.** Don't end with "Order yours today!" or "Click to buy!"
- **No emoji in body copy.** Emojis OK in social captions sparingly, never in product descriptions.
- **Avoid "I" or "we" too much.** Third person and passive voice are fine when they keep the focus on the object.

## Structure templates

### Product description (most common)

```
[Era + form, 1 sentence]. [Provenance / story / who made this, 1-2 sentences]. [Condition + dimensions, 1-2 sentences]. [Practical note  -  use, care, pairing, 1 sentence].
```

Example:

> 1954 Numechron Tele-Vision flip clock in Bakelite case. Manufactured by Numechron Inc. for General Time, recognizable by its mechanical flip-card mechanism and television-set styling that mirrored mid-century optimism about household electronics. Working order; minor scuffing on the case consistent with seven decades of bedside duty. Plug it in next to a brass lamp and a stack of mystery paperbacks.

### Category description (one sentence, scene-setting)

```
[Curatorial premise  -  what lives in this room and why we keep it].
```

Examples:

> The Apothecary Cabinet  -  Sages, salves, and remedies for the calmer corners of the day.
> The Vintage Market  -  Pre-loved objects with paperwork: provenance, era, and the wear they've earned.
> The Real Rarities  -  Things we acquired once and won't acquire again.

### Tagline / hero

Keep them short and a touch literary.

- "A modern mercantile" (existing tagline  -  keep using)
- "For the next collector"
- "Stocked by hand"
- "Every item has paperwork"

### Email subject lines

Plain, specific, no exclamation marks.

- "Three pieces from an Iowa estate, just arrived"
- "The flip clock is back"
- "Open Saturday and Sunday this weekend"

### Social caption

One observation, one detail, one invitation. No hashtag stuffing.

> "Found this in a barn near Charles City. The label is original; the patina is sixty years earned. In the shop now under The Real Rarities."

## Voice examples  -  rewriting

| Don't | Do |
|---|---|
| "Amazing vintage clock  -  must have for any collector!" | "1954 Numechron flip clock. Working order. Original cord, minor case wear." |
| "Get yours before they're gone!" | "We acquired three; two remain." |
| "Beautiful sage bundle for cleansing your space" | "Hand-tied white sage with Sinuata flowers. 4-inch bundles. Hand-tied in the tradition of frontier healers." |
| "Stunning Victorian doll, perfect gift!" | "Gustel Wied collector doll, German manufacture, 1980s. Original box and tag. The dress is uncreased." |
| "Brand new arrivals from our latest haul!" | "Newly arrived from an Iowa estate sale, week of May 4. Eleven pieces; nine remain." |
| "Don't miss our sale!" | "Sage bundles, 15% off this week. We rarely discount, but we received more than we expected." |

## Voice across surfaces

| Surface | Approach |
|---|---|
| Product description | Full template (era -> provenance -> condition -> use) |
| Item card mini-description | First two sentences of the full description |
| Category page intro | Single curatorial sentence |
| Marketing email | Plain subject + body that reads like a letter from the shopkeeper |
| Social caption | One observation + one detail + soft invitation |
| Push notification | "Newly arrived: [Era + form]"  -  never "FLASH SALE!" |
| Receipt / order confirmation | Warm + factual. "Thank you for your order. Your [item] was packed by hand this morning and ships tomorrow." |

## Procedure for any writing task

1. **Identify the object.** What is the item / category / topic? Name it specifically.
2. **Anchor the era or context.** What year, what maker, what cultural moment?
3. **Note the condition or scarcity.** Don't gloss this.
4. **End with a use.** How might the buyer live with this?
5. **Cut hype words.** Read it back and delete any "amazing," "perfect," "must-have."
6. **Read it aloud.** If it sounds like a 1920s shopkeeper writing a hand-lettered card, you're there. If it sounds like a Shopify template, rewrite.
